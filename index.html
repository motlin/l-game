4-player battle rules

  1. The board is 6 by 6. Four players each own an L, and there are four black blockers that belong to nobody. The L's start locked together in the middle, each a quarter turn of the next, so the board itself favors nobody. Moving early is still an edge, so the first player rotates every match.
  2. Turns go clockwise. On your turn you must move your L to a new spot, then you may move one blocker to any empty square, same as the classic game.
  3. An L is checkmated when its own turn starts and it has no legal place to move: it turns to stone and stays on the board as a statue for the rest of the match, getting in everyone's way. The player who moved just before scores the checkmate. You can never be checkmated while it's someone else's turn.
  4. The floor collapses. After turn 10, a tile somewhere on the board starts cracking: it can be anywhere, even under an L, a statue or a blocker. No L can move onto it, but you can drop a blocker on it. It falls away once every player still in play has moved one more time, so you always get a move to get your L off it. A blocker on it falls too and drops back from the sky onto a random empty square. If the floor gives way under a statue, that square of the statue sinks into the gap and becomes grey stone floor: anyone can stand on it, and it can crack and fall again later, and then it really is a hole. So statues can shrink into odd shapes like a straight line of three or a tiny L. Then the next tile starts cracking. If the shrinking floor is what left an L with nowhere to go when its turn starts, it turns to stone by natural causes, and every player still standing scores a point.
  5. The last L standing wins. The match ends when only one L can still move, or when you press End match (then nobody wins). If the last L's all turn to stone at the same moment, they share the win.
  6. The crown just shows who has the most checkmates among the L's still in play. A statue can never wear it.
  7. Optional hammers (turn them on under the board): every 3 rounds (a round is one turn for every player) a hammer appears on an empty square (up to 3 at once), and one nobody picks up crumbles away after 5 rounds. Move your L onto it to pick it up. The next time you set your L down, it may cover one statue square, and that piece of the statue breaks away. Or it may cover squares of another player's L: each hammer breaks one square, as long as what's left of that L stays in one piece, so a piece shrinks to 3 squares, then 2, then 1. A smaller piece must keep its new shape, and it fits in more places, so it's harder to box in. Breaking a player's last square knocks them out of the game, which counts as a checkmate for the player with the hammer. A hammer can also smash a blocker your L lands on; the blocker drops back down on a random empty square. If your L lands on a hammer and something breakable in the same move, the hammer is picked up and used on it straight away, so that's never a dead end. A hammer on a tile that falls is lost.
  8. L vs T teams: players 1 and 3 are the L team (same color) and players 2 and 4 are the T team, taking turns L, T, L, T. Teammates can't break each other with hammers, and boxing in your own teammate scores nothing. A team wins when both players on the other team are out.

Manual: good and bad positions

Why it's hard to beat three computers

You start with a 1-in-4 chance. When four Hard computers play each other, each seat wins about a quarter of the time. In test matches, a player at the Easy computer's level won 2 of 14 (14%) and went out first in 36%.

The main difference is space. The computers count every place each L can move to, on every move. They kept about 11 places on average, while the Easy-level player had 8.8. Fewer places means fewer ways out when someone closes in. The scoreboard now shows that count for every L, so you can play by it too.

Strategy guide: 4-player battle

Is the 4-player game winnable?

Checkmates happen in almost every match. In 112 matches between computer players, all but one had at least one checkmate, and Hard players usually landed the first one within about 8 turns.

Being the last L standing: now guaranteed. Without a time limit, not one of those matches got down to a single survivor: once two L's are left, careful play can dodge forever, like in the classic game. So after turn 10 the floor starts falling away, one tile per turn, until only one L can move.

Can anyone force a win? Nobody knows. The classic game has 36,736 positions and is fully solved. This one has roughly 690 billion (about 172 billion board layouts, times four players who could be next), far too many to solve. And with three opponents, any lead can be ganged up on.

Turn order matters. When Hard computers played each other (under an earlier most-checkmates rule), players 1 and 2 came out on top in 27 of 36 matches, even though the starting position is perfectly symmetric. So the first player now rotates every match.

Position codes for 4 players

Any battle position fits in 21 characters (plus any holes and the score), like . It works like the classic code, with chess-style squares.

  1. Squares: a column letter A to F (left to right) and a row number 1 to 6 (top to bottom), so the top-left square is A1.
  2. First character: whose turn it is, 1 to 4.
  3. Next, four L's in player order, three characters each: the corner square, then the same direction digit as the classic code. Stand on the corner facing along the long arm: up 1 or 2, right 3 or 4, down 5 or 6, left 7 or 8. Odd if the foot sticks out to your right, even if to your left. A lowercase letter means that L is a statue.
  4. Last, the four blocker squares, two characters each, in alphabetical order.
  5. Holes (optional): a / followed by the squares that have fallen away, once the floor has started collapsing. A statue loses any of its squares that are holes, so the code still describes crumbled statues exactly.
  6. Stone floor (optional): a ~ followed by the grey squares where part of a statue sank and became floor. Squares of a statue that are also listed here count as sunk, so they're gone from it.
  7. Statue on stone (optional, rare): a ^ followed by squares where a statue stands on top of stone floor (an L that turned to stone on a square another statue had sunk into). Example: ^ C3.
  8. Optional score: a + and four digits for each player's checkmates, added once anyone has scored.

Ways you can lose

Real checkmates from computer matches. The trapped L is marked with ✕. Grey L's are statues.

Across 207 checkmates in those matches, the trapped L was touching a blocker 90% of the time, an edge 76%, a statue 37%, the middle 2×2 36% and a corner 34%.

Winning strategies

  • Take every checkmate you see. Each one removes a rival and gets you closer to being the last L standing, and the crown marks you as the deadliest player still in play.
  • Target the leader, but only when it pays. The crown holder has proven they can trap people, so they are the biggest threat. But when one computer hunted the leader all game, it came out on top 8 times in 36, no better than its fair share of 9: hunting dragged the leader down and helped everyone else. Squeeze the leader when the same move also sets up your own checkmate.
  • Use blockers to close the last exit. 90% of trapped L's had a blocker touching them. Find the player with the fewest moves and drop a blocker into their last gap.
  • Turn people to stone where it hurts others. A statue is a permanent wall. Trap someone far from your own L, ideally between your rivals.

Losing habits

  • Hugging the edge. 76% of trapped L's touched an edge: half your exits are gone before anyone moves.
  • Parking next to a statue. It never moves, so it is a wall you can never get back. 37% of trapped L's had one beside them.
  • Trusting the middle. In the classic game the center is safe. Here it fills up with L's and statues, and 36% of trapped L's touched it. Look for open space, not just the center.
  • Leaving a blocker next to yourself. Move it away when you can, or someone else will use it against you.
  • Wearing the crown carelessly. It makes you everyone's target. When you are ahead, pick moves that keep the most places open for your L.

How to play

  1. You each own one L. Yours is the one. The two black discs belong to nobody.
  2. Every turn, you must move your L to a new spot. Swipe across four empty squares in an L shape. You can turn or flip it.
  3. Then, if you like, move one black disc to any empty square to get in your opponent's way. Or just end your turn.
  4. You lose when your L has nowhere to go at the start of your turn. Box your opponent in before they box you in.

Your first match is a short lesson against the Perfect computer. It is set up so you will lose in a few moves, so you can see exactly how losing works. After that you will play the Easy computer.

Computer strength

Moving Tiles rules

  1. The same L game, but the floor itself moves. Two players start from the classic 4 by 4 opening with 2 blockers. Four players start from the battle pinwheel on 6 by 6, with 4 blockers.
  2. On your turn: move your L (you must), then you may move one blocker, then you may move one tile.
  3. You can lift any tile that has no L on it. A blocker on the tile rides along with it, and so does a one-piece statue (a statue with only one block left). Tiles under bigger statues stay put. Set the tile down on an empty spot that shares a side with at least one other tile. Touching only at a corner doesn't count. The floor must also stay one connected piece: a move that would leave any tile or group of tiles cut off (or joined only at a corner) isn't allowed. Grey stone tiles can be moved too. The board can grow and change shape, but only so far.
  4. A one-piece statue can also be moved like a blocker: in the blocker step, pick it up and put it on any empty tile.
  5. Taking a tile away from next to someone is a great way to box them in, and adding one can open an escape route.
  6. An L with no legal move at the start of its turn is out. With two players, the other player wins, and the boxed-in L is marked with X's as in the classic game.
  7. No blockers (switch under the board): the match starts without any blockers, so each turn is just your L and then a tile.
  8. L vs T works here too. With 2 players it's Player 1's L against Player 2's T on the 4 by 4 floor, with one blocker in the corner. With 4 players it's two L's against two T's as teams: teammates share a color, can't hammer each other, and a team wins when the other team is out.
  9. With hammers on (2 or 4 players), a hammer can chip ends off another player's L until it's a single square, and breaking that last square knocks them out. With two players that's a second way to win: box in the other L, or smash it completely.
  10. With four players the battle rules apply: statues, checkmate points, the crown, and the collapsing floor after turn 10. A cracking tile can't be moved; it just falls away when its time comes.
  11. The floor only cracks where losing that tile keeps it in one piece. Only when no tile like that is left can a crack split the floor. Then every island except the biggest drifts away, taking its blockers (they drop back onto the main island), statues and any L standing on it. An L carried off is out, and everyone still standing gets +1.

Order the ways

Position codes

Any position fits in six characters, like C5 N1 AP: two for the first player's L, two for the second player's L, and two for the discs. Once you learn the three rules below you can read or write a code by eye, and set up any position you like to try your own variants.

1. Squares are letters

The board is lettered A to P, left to right, top to bottom, like reading a page.

2. An L is a corner and a direction

The corner is the square where the short foot joins the long arm (marked with a dot). Write its letter, then a digit for the way the long arm runs from it: up 1 or 2, right 3 or 4, down 5 or 6, left 7 or 8. Stand on the corner facing along the arm: if the foot sticks out to your right, use the odd digit; to your left, the even one.

3. The discs say whose turn it is

The last two letters are the two disc squares. Written in alphabetical order (like AP), it is the first player's turn. Written backwards (PA), it is the second player's turn.

Worked example: the standard opening

C5: the first player's corner is C and the arm runs down (5 or 6). Facing down from C, the foot on B is to your right, so 5. N1: the second player's corner is N, the arm runs up, and the foot on O is to the right, so 1. AP: discs on A and P, in alphabetical order, so the first player moves.

How to play

  1. The board is 4 by 4. Each player owns one L-shaped piece covering four squares, and two black neutral discs belong to nobody.
  2. On your turn you must pick up your L and set it down in a different position. You can slide, turn or flip it, and it may reuse some of its old squares, but it can't cover the other L or a disc. Here you place it by swiping across its four squares in one stroke; let go early and the stroke is discarded so you can redraw.
  3. Then you may move one neutral disc to any empty square, or leave both where they are.
  4. If you can't move your L when your turn starts, you lose.

The game is small enough to solve completely: this page works out all 18,368 positions when it loads. From the starting position perfect play is a draw, so the Perfect computer never loses. Beat Medium first, then try to survive Perfect and pounce if it ever lets you.
